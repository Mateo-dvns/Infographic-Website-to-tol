Design Psychology in Websites

How good design shapes what your customer feels, before they read a single word.

Good design is felt before it is read. This infographic website shows how first impressions, color, and a few key principles shape what a customer feels, and why designers must use that power responsibly.

50 ms

The First 50 Milliseconds

Before a visitor reads a single word, they have already made up their mind. Research by Lindgaard and colleagues (2006) found that people form a lasting opinion of a website’s visual appeal in as little as 50 milliseconds, about 0.05 seconds. That’s a gut reaction, not a considered judgment.

Feels safe: A clean, balanced layout builds trust.

Feels risky: A cluttered or clashing layout creates doubt and anxiety.

Color Psychology: What Each Color Says

Six colors, six different feelings.

Red

Creates instant urgency and grabs attention.

  • Emotional effects: Passion, excitement, urgency.
  • Body and mind: Often linked with alertness and a sense of energy.
  • Customer behavior: Encourages impulse buying, signals scarcity, commands attention.
  • Common use: Clearance sales, fast-food ordering, flash deals, main Call-To-Action (CTA) buttons.

Blue

Builds trust by projecting stability and calm.

  • Emotional effects: Trust, dependability, serenity, security.
  • Body and mind: Commonly associated with calm and focus.
  • Customer behavior: Eases anxiety during high-friction steps and supports retention.
  • Common use: Banks, healthcare portals, tech platforms, payment gateways.

Brand examples:

Yellow

Catches the eye faster than most colors.

  • Emotional effects: Confidence, optimism, energy.
  • Body and mind: Very bright yellow can tire the eyes.
  • Customer behavior: Prompts quick, decisive action.
  • Common use: Warning signs, traffic lights, safety vests, school buses.

Brand examples:

Purple

Associated with awe, status, and exclusivity.

  • Emotional effects: Calm (soft shades like lavender), creativity, mystery.
  • Body and mind: Rarely found in natural foods, so it's less tied to appetite.
  • Customer behavior: Signals premium quality and higher price.
  • Common use: Creative agencies, beauty and luxury brands.

Brand examples:

Green

Signals growth, health, and “go.”

  • Emotional effects: Balance, safety, freshness, renewal.
  • Body and mind: Easy on the eyes and often seen as restful.
  • Customer behavior: Reassures users that an action is safe or successful.
  • Common use: Wellness brands, eco products, success messages, “Confirm” buttons, finance apps.

Brand examples:

Orange

Friendly and energetic, a softer call to action than red.

  • Emotional effects: Enthusiasm, warmth, playfulness, optimism.
  • Body and mind: Feels lively without the intensity of red.
  • Customer behavior: Encourages action without pressure.
  • Common use: Sign-up buttons, youth brands, food and entertainment sites.

Reality Check

Color psychology is a guide, not a guarantee. Meaning shifts with culture (white signals mourning in parts of Asia, while red means luck in China), with context (red means “sale” in a store but “error” in a form), and with personal experience. Use color to support your message, never to replace good content.

Key Principles Behind the Click

Psychology and design overlap in many ways. These four principles show up everywhere, and each one connects back to how a visitor feels.

Hick's Law

More choices, slower decisions.

The idea: The time it takes to decide grows with the number of options. Psychologists William Hick (1952) and Ray Hyman (1953) found this while studying reaction times.

How it feels: Overwhelmed, stressed, tempted to leave.

Real example: A search page with one box and one button feels calm. A homepage with 40 links and 6 banners feels like work.

Design tip: Keep the menu short and give every page one clear main action.

Aesthetic-Usability Effect

Beautiful feels easier.

The idea: People tend to see good-looking designs as easier to use. In a well-known 1995 study, Kurosu and Kashimura found that people rated more attractive ATM layouts as easier to use, even when the layouts worked the same.

How it feels: Patient, forgiving, confident.

Real example: A polished checkout page makes a small hiccup feel like a minor glitch. On an ugly page, the same hiccup feels like a broken site.

Design tip: Spend time on spacing, alignment, and consistent colors. Polish earns goodwill.

Von Restorff Effect

The odd one out gets remembered.

The idea: When one item looks different from the rest, people notice it and remember it. It's named after psychologist Hedwig von Restorff, who studied it in 1933.

How it feels: Curious, drawn in, guided.

Real example: Imagine a page of blue and gray buttons with one bright orange "Sign up." Your eye goes straight to it. Online stores do this with their "Add to cart" button.

Design tip: Save your boldest color for the one action you want most. If everything stands out, nothing does.

Jakob's Law

Familiar feels safe.

The idea: Users spend most of their time on other websites, so they expect yours to work the same way. Usability expert Jakob Nielsen described this.

How it feels: Comfortable, in control, unthinking (in a good way).

Real example: Logo at the top left, search at the top, cart at the top right. You never had to be taught this, because nearly every shop works that way.

Design tip: Be creative with visuals, but keep basic patterns where people expect them.

Website Example: Cluttered vs. Clean

Cluttered homepage

40 menu links, 6 banners, 3 pop-ups, and 5 competing buttons. Visitors don't know where to look, resulting people to leave the website.

Clean homepageeeee

One headline, one search box, one button. Visitors know what to do in seconds.

Good design isn't adding more. It's removing what gets in the way.

The Ethics of Emotional Design

Knowing how emotions work comes with responsibility. Designers can guide users, but they can also manipulate them. Dark patterns include fake countdown timers that borrow red’s urgency, guilt-trip buttons (“No thanks, I don’t like saving money”), and hidden costs.

Ethical design uses psychology to make decisions easier for the user, not to trick them into choices they wouldn’t otherwise make.

Takeaways

Good design is felt before it is understood.

Win the first glance. You have 50 milliseconds. Match color to message. Let the feeling fit the goal. Respect the user. Guide choices, never trick them.
