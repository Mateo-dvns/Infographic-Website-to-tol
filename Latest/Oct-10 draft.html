There are a number of debates about which additional skills designers should learn. Should designers code, write, or understand business? These skills are incredibly valuable but perhaps not essential . However, I would argue that every designer should learn the fundamentals of psychology.

As humans, we have an underlying “blueprint” for how we perceive and process the world around us, and the study of psychology helps us define this blueprint. As designers, we can leverage psychology to build more intuitive, human-centered products and experiences. Instead of forcing users to conform to the design of a product or experience, we can use some key principles from psychology as a guide for designing how people actually are. But knowing where to start can be a challenge. Which principles from psychology are useful? What are some examples of these principles at work? In this article, i'll cover the basics, and discuss the ethical implications of using psychology in design.

Section 2: Color Psychology

Red

Creates instant urgency and stimulates appetite by raising your physical heart rate.

Details & Impact

  • Emotional Effects: Triggers passion, excitement, urgency, and survival instincts.
  • Physical Effects: Raises heart rate, elevates blood pressure, and stimulates appetite.
  • Customer Behavior: Drives impulse buying, creates a sense of scarcity, and commands immediate attention.
  • Common Use Cases: E-commerce clearance sales, fast food ordering platforms, flash deals, and primary Call-To-Action (CTA) buttons.

Blue

Establishes subconscious trust and security by projecting stability and professional calm.

Details and Impacts

  • Emotional Effects: Induces feelings of trust, dependability, serenity, and security.
  • Physical Effects: Lowers pulse rate and body temperature, creating a calming physiological effect.
  • Customer Behavior: Reduces anxiety during high-friction steps and increases platform retention.
  • Common Use Cases: Financial institutions, healthcare portals, tech platforms, and online payment gateways.

Yellow

Yellow captures human attention faster than any other color

Details and Impacts

  • Emotional Effects: Confidence It stimulates self-esteem and boosts personal willpower.
  • Physical Effects: Eye Fatigue Bright yellow overstimulates the retina and strains eyes.
  • Customer Behavior: Quick Decisiveness It prompts fast actions and speedy buying decisions
  • Common Use Cases: Safety Signs Traffic lights, vests, and school buses maximize visibility.

Purple

people's first reactions to the color purple involved awe, high status, and exclusivity

Details and Impacts

  • Emotional Effects: Calmness Soft shades like lavender reduce mental stress.
  • physical effects: Appetite Suppression It rarely appears in food, reducing hunger drives.
  • Customer Behavior: Luxury Spending People associate purple with premium quality and high cost.
  • Common Use Cases: Creative Agencies: Logos adopt purple to show cutting-edge original thinking.
Design Psychology in Websites

Key principles

The intersection of psychology and design is extensive. Theres an endless list of principles that occupy this space but there are a few that i've found more ubiquitous than others. Lets take a look at what these are and where they are effectively leveraged by products and experiences we interact with everyday.

Hick's Law

One of the primary functions we have as designers is to synthesize information and present it in a way that it doesnt overwhelm users after all, good communication strives for clarity. This directly relates to our first key principle: Hicks Law. Hicks Law predicts that the time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available. It was formulated by psychologists William Edmund Hick and Ray Hyman in 1952 after examining the relationship between the number of stimuli present and an individuals reaction time to any given stimulus.


There are examples of Hicks Law in action everywhere, but well start with a common one: remote controls. As features available in TVs increased over the decades, so did the options available on their corresponding remotes. Eventually we ended up with remotes so complex that using them required either muscle memory from repeated use or a significant amount of mental processing. This led to the phenomenon known as “grandparent-friendly remote.” By taping off everything except for the essential buttons, grandkids were able to improve the usability of remotes for their loved ones, and they also did us all the favor of sharing them online.
